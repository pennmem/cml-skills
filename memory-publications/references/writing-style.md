# Writing Style Profile: Michael J. Kahana

Extracted from close reading of published works including *Computational Models of Memory Search* (Annual Review of Psychology, 2020), *EEG Decoders Track Memory Dynamics* (Nature Communications, 2024), *The Penn Electrophysiology of Encoding and Retrieval Study* (JEP:LMC, 2024), and additional publications. Consult this file before drafting any prose.

---

## 1. Opening & Framing

Kahana's openings are distinctive. He rarely begins with a bland summary of the field. Instead, he uses one of these strategies:

**Historical vignette or quotation that reframes the problem.** The Annual Review paper opens by quoting Tulving & Madigan (1970) at length — a passage that was both humorous and prescient — then pivots to show how their "outrageous prospects" are now within reach. This move grounds current work in the long arc of the field while making the reader feel they're witnessing intellectual progress.

**Direct statement of the core scientific question, then immediate engagement.** The Nature Communications paper opens: "Cognitive neuroscience has experienced a paradigm shift." No hedging, no multi-paragraph buildup. Within two sentences it's into the specific methodology and theoretical motivation.

**Narrative framing that situates a project in a larger scientific story.** The PEERS paper opens with Ebbinghaus, then contrasts standard single-session studies with the intensive-measurement approach: "This approach contrasts with research areas such as perception, where a small handful of observers often contribute data across a dozen or more experimental sessions." The paper then frames PEERS as an adventure: "here we tell the story of PEERS: its genesis, evolution, major findings, and the lessons it taught us about taking a big scientific approach."

**Candid acknowledgment of how things actually happened.** A distinctive Kahana move is the honest aside about the gap between ideal planning and reality: "Although one might hope that this major experimental endeavor resulted from a careful planning exercise, with scholars representing diverse interests advising as to the optimal choice of tasks, the initial choice of tasks arose from a desire to obtain high-resolution data on a set of tasks that had fueled our lab's theoretical work on human memory." This kind of candor builds trust with the reader.

**Pattern to follow:** Open with something that gives the reader a reason to care — an intellectual surprise, a concrete phenomenon, a historical connection. Avoid "Memory is an important cognitive function..." generalities.

**Textbook openings with classical quotations.** In *Foundations of Human Memory*, chapters open with epigraphs from classical sources (Aristotle's *De Memoria et Reminiscentia* in Chapter 8, Ebbinghaus' *On Memory* in Chapter 1, Ebbinghaus and Woodworth together in Chapter 9), followed by an accessible entry point that grounds the topic in everyday experience: "It is hard to imagine a life without memory. Our memories define who we are, and our ability to learn and make new memories largely determines who we become." The book's very first substantive paragraph moves from this universal statement immediately to concrete examples (performing daily acts, communicating, learning words) before pivoting to why understanding memory matters for society.

**Historical narrative as intellectual scaffolding.** Chapter 1 builds an entire narrative arc from Ebbinghaus' self-experimentation through Müller's methodological improvements to Bower's temporal context model, treating the history of the field as a story of progressively more rigorous and abstract thinking. Each historical figure is introduced as a character with motivations and contributions, not just a citation.

## 2. Sentence Architecture

### Length and rhythm
Kahana writes in a mix of sentence lengths, but leans toward medium-to-long sentences (20-35 words) with occasional short declarative punctuation. He does not write in a clipped, telegraphic style. Sentences often contain embedded clauses that add precision:

> "Because context is always of unit length, it can be thought of as a point on a hypersphere, with β determining the distance it travels with the newly presented item and M^FC f_i determining the direction of travel."

This sentence is 40+ words but reads clearly because the clauses build on each other logically.

### Sentence-initial patterns
- Frequently opens with temporal/logical connectors: "Because...", "Whereas...", "Although...", "To the extent that...", "By contrast,...", "Rather than..."
- Uses "we" for empirical claims about the author's own work, but "one" or passive constructions for general methodological points
- Rarely starts with "It is..." or "There are..." — prefers active constructions

### Characteristic constructions
- **The "X, however, Y" pivot**: "The present reader will recognize that neural modulation has become a major therapeutic approach..., yet even as we control neural firing..., our core theoretical understanding is still firmly grounded in..."
- **The clarifying parenthetical**: Heavy use of em-dashes and parenthetical asides to add precision: "(the nondigital precursor to morphing faces)", "(i.e., the average of contexts experienced prior to the study list)"
- **The "To the extent that... we would expect..." conditional**: A signature construction linking theory to prediction

## 3. Evidence Integration

### How citations are introduced
Kahana typically **leads with the finding or idea, then cites**. He does not write "Smith (2005) found that X." Instead:

> "Analyses of retrieval dynamics provide particularly strong evidence for cue-dependent retrieval: Following recall of a given item, subjects tend to recall items with similar temporal, semantic, source, spatial, or affective attributes."

The citation comes in the surrounding context, not as the grammatical subject. When a specific researcher's contribution must be highlighted, he uses a more natural integration:

> "Polyn et al. (2009) introduced this initialization to model the effects of semantic organization on memory."

### Multiple citations
When citing several papers for a single claim, Kahana generally uses parenthetical citation at the end of the sentence or clause. He does not string together narrative citations ("A found X, B found Y, C found Z"). Instead, the finding is stated once and multiple citations support it.

### Self-citation
References his own prior work naturally, without false modesty or excessive self-promotion. Uses "we" for collaborative work, "I" rarely (but it appears in the Annual Review piece, which is single-authored).

## 4. Technical Register & Formalism

### Mathematical notation in prose
Kahana integrates mathematical notation directly into running text with careful verbal scaffolding:

> "Let f_i ∈ R^N represent the memorial representation of item i. The elements of f_i, denoted f_i(1), f_i(2), ..., f_i(n), may represent information in either a localist or a distributed manner."

He introduces notation verbally *before* the formal definition, then uses the notation fluidly afterward. Equations are never dropped in without explanation — each has a preceding sentence that tells the reader what the equation will express.

### Definitions and terminology
Defines technical terms precisely on first use, often in margin notes (Annual Review format) or inline. Does not assume the reader knows specialized abbreviations. But once defined, he uses abbreviations freely.

### Level of assumed knowledge
Assumes the reader is an educated scientist but not necessarily a specialist in computational memory. Explains why things matter, not just what they are. For instance, he doesn't just define the contiguity effect — he explains why it constrains models.

## 5. Hedging vs. Assertion

### When confident
Uses direct, unhedged language for well-established findings:

> "Subjects tend to make transitions among contiguous items, and do so with a forward bias."

> "These findings link neural variability predictive of successful memory with item-to-context binding, a key cognitive process thought to underlie episodic memory function."

### When uncertain or speculative
Deploys measured hedging without excessive qualification:

> "It is possible that other competitive choice models, or nonlinear network models, could also enable RCT to account for IRT data."

> "Although this idea has not been rigorously explored, it is analogous to..."

He avoids the pattern "It may perhaps be the case that..." — one hedge per uncertain claim is the norm.

### Characteristic confidence markers
- "The data indicate that..."
- "Consistent with this prediction..."
- "These findings demonstrate that..."
- "We can be certain that..."

### Characteristic uncertainty markers
- "It is likely that..."
- "One can easily imagine..."
- "These models are thus challenged by..."
- "To the extent that..."

## 6. Paragraph Structure

### Topic sentences
Paragraphs almost always open with a clear topic sentence that states the point. The rest of the paragraph provides evidence, elaboration, or mathematical formalization.

### Transitions between paragraphs
Transitions are typically logical rather than formulaic. He uses the end of one paragraph to set up the question or tension that the next paragraph addresses. Rarely uses mechanical transition phrases like "Furthermore," or "In addition," as paragraph openers — prefers "By contrast," "Whereas," "Although," or a direct statement that creates implicit contrast.

### Paragraph length
Tends toward medium-to-long paragraphs (6-12 sentences) in review writing. Empirical papers have shorter paragraphs, especially in Results sections.

---

## 7. Discussion Style

### How findings are interpreted
In Discussion sections, Kahana connects findings back to the theoretical framework quickly. He does not repeat the Results section. Instead, he immediately interprets:

> "We show that models based on spectral EEG features can reliably distinguish between the encoding of subsequently recalled and forgotten items..."

Then moves to implications, alternative explanations, and connections to prior work.

### Limitations
Acknowledges limitations honestly and specifically, without excessive self-flagellation. Does not devote an entire subsection to limitations — they're woven into the relevant discussion.

### Future directions
Specific and grounded, not vague: "These models will allow theorists to make specific predictions about how subjects recall naturally occurring events, such as narratives and films."

## 8. Distinctive Vocabulary & Phrasing

### Words and phrases Kahana frequently uses:
- "benchmark" (as adjective: "benchmark findings," "benchmark phenomena")
- "accounts for" (not "explains" — models "account for" data)
- "gives rise to" (not "causes" or "leads to")
- "insofar as"
- "by contrast" (not "on the other hand")
- "the foregoing"
- "whimsically"
- "elegantly" (for particularly clean theoretical moves)
- "formidable" (for serious theoretical challenges)
- "the hallmark of" / "embodies"
- "eschews" (models "eschew" complexity)
- "in the same manner that..."
- "the key theoretical ideas that have emerged"

### Words and phrases to avoid (not in Kahana's register):
- "interestingly" (almost never used)
- "importantly" (rarely)
- "it is worth noting that" (never)
- "in this paper, we" as an opening
- "the results suggest" without specification of what they suggest
- Excessive use of "novel" or "for the first time"
- "robust" as a generic intensifier

## 9. Formatting & Structural Preferences

### Section numbering
Uses numbered sections in review articles (1. INTRODUCTION, 2. MODELS FOR MEMORY, etc.). Empirical papers follow journal conventions.

### Figure references
References figures inline with clear direction about what to observe: "Figure 4a shows this measure, termed the lag-CRP, in both DFR and CDFR conditions." Always tells the reader what the figure demonstrates.

### Footnotes
Uses footnotes sparingly but substantively — typically for important qualifications or historical notes that would interrupt the main flow.

## 10. Meta-Scientific Voice

A distinctive feature of Kahana's writing, especially in larger-scale papers, is a reflective register that comments on the practice of science itself:

**Portfolio metaphor for scientific investment:** The PEERS paper explicitly borrows from finance — "portfolio choice" and "portfolio allocation" applied to how scientists (and granting agencies) should distribute resources across projects. This cross-domain thinking is characteristic: applying formal concepts from one field to illuminate another.

**Big-picture framing of methodological choices:** Rather than just describing what was done, he explains *why* certain approaches matter for the field: "Think of big data as a kind of microscope that allows you to zoom into a phenomenon and see structure that was previously obscured within the error bars of our small experiments."

**Engagement with critics:** When addressing objections to his approach, he does so with intellectual honesty: "After being tongue-tied for a few moments (or longer), the presenter recalled many instances in his past research where additional data revealed some important result via a new 'cut' of the data space." This kind of self-deprecating honesty is rare in scientific writing and very effective.

**Lessons-learned sections:** The PEERS paper includes an explicit "Lessons Learned" section that reads more like a lab manual than a journal article — practical advice on subject recruitment, data quality control, annotation of vocal responses. This didactic generosity toward future researchers is characteristic.

## 11. Cross-Domain Analogies & Metaphors

Kahana regularly reaches outside memory science for clarifying analogies:

- Plato's wax tablet, the Rabbis' papyrus sheet (Avot 4:20) — for memory encoding
- Robert Hooke's geometric model — foreshadowing modern cognitive models
- Galton's composite portraiture — for composite memory storage
- Dartboard analogy — for probabilistic sampling in SAM
- Hypersphere — for context space geometry
- Microscope/telescope — for big data's ability to reveal hidden structure
- Corporate portfolio allocation — for scientific investment decisions
- "Mental cul-de-sac" — for retrieval failure states

These analogies serve a specific purpose: they make formal mathematical concepts accessible without dumbing them down. When drafting for Kahana, reach for analogies from history, physics, economics, or everyday experience to illuminate abstract theoretical mechanisms.

**Real-world examples as evidence.** In the textbook, empirical points are regularly illustrated with vivid real-world scenarios: Godden & Baddeley's scuba-diver context experiment, Singh et al.'s television commercial spacing study, Jenkins & Dallenbach's Cornell students sleeping in a laboratory dormitory. These are told with narrative detail ("They paid two Cornell University seniors to live in an improvised dormitory attached to the experimental psychology laboratory for seven weeks"), not just cited.

**Quotations from outside psychology.** Robert Lucas (Nobel economist): "It is, at least for me, the working of highly abstract but explicit models that is the source of ideas..." Radossawljevitch's confused subject who read syllables 46 times without realizing he was supposed to memorize them. William James reviewing Ebbinghaus in *Science*. These cross-domain voices enliven the text and situate memory science within the broader intellectual landscape.

## 12. APA/JEP-Specific Conventions

Based on the PEERS paper (published in JEP:LMC), which is a particularly good exemplar of Kahana's APA writing:

**Abstract style:** Opens with the study name and aim, then states results and implications in concrete terms. No filler — every sentence does work.

**Use of "we":** Consistently "we" for the research team's actions, decisions, and findings. "We pursued three primary aims." "We achieved these goals." "We feel that it is now time to pull together the as-yet-incomplete PEERS story." In the textbook, "we" is used inclusively with the reader: "we consider the basic experimental data on memory for sequences."

**Figure references in text:** Always tells the reader what to look at and why: "Figure 2C shows the striking similarity in recall initiation across immediate and continual-distractor free recall." Never just "(see Figure 2)."

**Footnotes:** Used for important qualifications that would derail the paragraph flow, or for brief methodological notes. In the textbook, footnotes explain scoring methods, historical terminology, or alternative interpretations that would interrupt the main narrative.

**Abbreviation discipline:** Defines on first use, then uses consistently. But doesn't abbreviate terms that only appear a few times.

## 12b. Pedagogical Register (Textbook Voice)

From *Foundations of Human Memory*, a distinctive teaching voice emerges that differs from the journal-article voice:

**Concrete running examples.** The book uses the same example list throughout a chapter — "house, shoe, tree, car, dog, key, rose, boat" — to illustrate increasingly complex concepts (positional clustering, temporal clustering, transposition errors). This thread of continuity helps the reader build understanding incrementally.

**"Consider..." as a transition device.** Frequently opens explanatory passages with "Consider the following experiment:" or "Consider, for instance, participants 15 and 16 who consistently paused after recalling items 3, 6, and 9." This invites the reader into active engagement rather than passive reception.

**Grounding formalism in intuition.** Before introducing a formal measure or analysis, the book walks through a concrete example: "To make this clustering measure concrete, consider the example list house, shoe, tree, car, ... boat. If a participant recalls house, shoe, tree, then tree is at distance 0 because it appears in the third position both in the list and in the recall sequence."

**Signposting across chapters.** Heavy use of forward and backward references: "In chapter 9, we will discuss both classes of models and the key data supporting each of them." "As in our discussion of free recall, we have seen that such dissociations appear even when..."

**Honest about what we don't know.** Doesn't pretend that the field has settled every question: "A major question that we have not yet addressed concerns the nature of the information being stored in serial-order memory tasks."

When drafting textbook-style or review content for Kahana, lean into this pedagogical voice: use running examples, "Consider..." invitations, and explicit connections between sections.

**Personal anecdotes in scholarly context.** In the textbook, personal experience occasionally surfaces to ground abstract concepts: "My great uncle often used to say, 'I remember it as if it were yesterday' when recounting in vivid detail a salient event that took place in his youth." These are brief, purposeful, and always transition immediately into the scientific point. In journal articles, this personal voice appears in different forms — the "tongue-tied" moment in the PEERS paper, the chuckle at Tulving & Madigan in the Annual Review.

**Structuring principles explicitly.** When presenting evaluative frameworks, Kahana states criteria directly and concisely. For example, four dimensions for evaluating a memory model: Accuracy, Generality, Testability, Parsimony — each with a one-sentence definition. This reflects a preference for making the intellectual scaffolding visible to the reader.

## 13. Voice Summary

Kahana writes with the voice of someone who has spent decades thinking carefully about his subject and wants to share that understanding with intellectual equals. The writing is:

- **Precise without being pedantic** — every technical term earns its place
- **Narrative without being casual** — there's a story arc even in dense theoretical sections
- **Confident without being dismissive** — he states what the data show clearly but acknowledges what we don't know
- **Historically grounded** — frequently connects current work to the intellectual lineage of the field (Ebbinghaus, Aristotle, the Rabbis, Plato, William James)
- **Model-driven** — even when discussing phenomena, the writing is oriented toward what models predict, what they account for, and where they fall short
- **Candidly reflective** — willing to acknowledge how things actually happened vs. how they might ideally have happened, which builds trust
- **Cross-disciplinary in metaphor** — reaches into economics, physics, and history for illuminating analogies

When drafting text for Kahana, aim for this voice: authoritative, historically aware, model-focused, and precisely worded. Prefer clarity over jargon, but don't shy away from formalism when it serves the argument. When writing narrative or discussion sections, lean into the reflective, storytelling register — especially for larger-scale papers where the arc of a research program matters as much as individual findings.
